Why Do the Hanish Islands Matter for Red Sea Shipping?

Houthi forces have seized Greater and Lesser Hanish in the southern Red Sea, strengthening their military position near the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint. That raises risks for shipping and oil flows, but it does not mean the strait is currently closed.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 14, 2026 · Multi-source verified synthesis

The immediate change is geographic. The Associated Press reported Monday that Houthi forces captured Greater and Lesser Hanish, islands positioned along the approaches to the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. The seizure follows earlier Houthi gains around Yemen's Red Sea coast and Mayun Island, putting more territory around the chokepoint under Houthi control.

Reuters independently reported that the Houthi advance has brought the group closer to one of the world's most important maritime routes and has increased pressure on Saudi Arabia and commercial shipping. The development matters because Bab el-Mandeb connects the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean, forming the southern gateway to the Suez Canal route.

Why are the Hanish Islands strategically important?

The islands sit close to shipping lanes used by vessels moving between the Red Sea and the open ocean. Holding them can improve a military force's ability to observe nearby traffic, position weapons or sensors, and threaten vessels operating in the area.

That does not by itself give the Houthis complete control over every ship transiting Bab el-Mandeb. Commercial traffic was still moving through the strait over the weekend. Reuters, citing preliminary ship-tracking data, reported 24 and 27 vessel transits on Saturday and Sunday, roughly in line with the recent 10-day average.

How much oil moves through Bab el-Mandeb?

The route has become more important during the broader disruption to Middle East energy flows. U.S. Energy Information Administration data show that about 8.1 million barrels per day of crude oil and petroleum liquids moved through Bab el-Mandeb in the second quarter of 2026, up from 5.6 million barrels per day in the first quarter.

A serious disruption could force some ships to take the much longer route around Africa's Cape of Good Hope. That can add travel time, fuel use and shipping costs even if oil production itself is unchanged.

Does the seizure mean Bab el-Mandeb is blocked?

No. The available evidence does not show a general closure of the strait. The key change is an increase in the Houthis' ability to exert pressure near the route, not proof that normal commercial passage has stopped.

The distinction matters because shipping markets respond not only to actual closures but also to the perceived risk of attacks, seizures, insurance losses or future restrictions. Individual carriers can reroute even while other ships continue through the chokepoint.

Why does this matter now?

Bab el-Mandeb is especially important because other Middle East energy routes have also faced disruption. Saudi Arabia has relied more heavily on Red Sea export routes when traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has been constrained, making security around Yemen's western coast more consequential for global energy markets.

The seizure also deepens Yemen's renewed conflict. Reuters reported that more than 82,000 people have been displaced since the latest Houthi advance began, adding a humanitarian cost to the strategic contest over the coast and islands.

What remains uncertain: The extent to which the Houthis will use the newly captured islands to interfere with commercial shipping is not yet established. Bab el-Mandeb remains open, vessel traffic has continued, and the future military response by Yemeni, Saudi or other forces remains unclear.
