Can Trump’s New USPS Mail-Ballot Rules Take Effect for the 2026 Midterms?

Not for now. Two federal judges have blocked the Postal Service from enforcing new ballot-mail requirements for the 2026 midterm elections, while the U.S. Supreme Court considers an emergency request that could change that status.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 14, 2026 · Multi-source verified synthesis

The immediate legal answer is that the new U.S. Postal Service ballot-mail rules are currently blocked. The Associated Press reported September 14 that U.S. District Judge Carl J. Nichols issued a preliminary injunction in Washington, becoming the second federal judge to prevent the requirements from taking effect before the November midterms. A separate Massachusetts injunction has already been upheld by the 1st U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals.

What would the USPS mail-ballot rules require?

The August rule would impose new federal requirements on states and local election offices that use the mail for federal ballots. The 1st Circuit described requirements that include obtaining Postal Service approval for ballot-envelope designs, using voter-specific Intelligent Mail barcodes and uploading voter information to a USPS system.

Under the rule as described by the court, ballots could be affected if the required barcode and uploaded voter information did not match. The Postal Service itself acknowledged in the rule that existing injunctions prevented implementation for the 2026 election unless the government obtained relief from those court orders.

Why have courts blocked the rules?

The Massachusetts case centers in part on the Constitution’s Elections Clause. The 1st Circuit said the challengers had shown a likelihood of success on their claim that the rule intrudes on authority the Constitution assigns primarily to states, subject to congressional regulation of federal elections. The appeals court also left in place factual findings that implementing the requirements shortly before the election could force states to redesign envelopes, build new technical processes and risk disruption to ballot delivery.

The administration has defended the changes as lawful Postal Service regulations intended to protect election integrity. Reuters reported September 10 that the administration asked courts to allow the requirements to take effect, while challengers argued that the executive branch was attempting to regulate election administration without authority from Congress.

What changed on September 14?

The new development is the injunction from Judge Nichols in the District of Columbia. Earlier in the litigation, Nichols had declined to block the president’s executive order because agencies had not yet issued the implementing regulations. AP reports that, after the Postal Service finalized those regulations in August, Nichols concluded that the challengers had now shown grounds for preliminary relief.

That means two separate federal cases now stand between the Postal Service and implementation of the new requirements for this year’s midterms.

Could the Supreme Court still allow the rules to take effect?

Yes. The Supreme Court is considering an emergency application arising from the Massachusetts litigation. A Supreme Court filing in that case confirms that the dispute concerns whether the Postal Service may apply the new ballot-mail requirements while the litigation continues.

The Court has not yet issued a final ruling resolving the legality of the regulations. An emergency order could change whether the rules operate during the 2026 election, while the underlying constitutional and statutory litigation could continue afterward.

What does this mean for voters right now?

The injunctions mean the challenged federal USPS requirements are not currently the rules voters should assume govern whether their 2026 mail ballots will be delivered. Voting-by-mail procedures still vary by state, and voters should follow the instructions and deadlines issued by their own state or local election officials.

What remains unresolved: The Supreme Court could still alter the rules’ status before the November 3 midterms. The current injunctions are preliminary and do not amount to a final nationwide Supreme Court decision on the underlying constitutional questions.
