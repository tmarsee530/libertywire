Why Did Trump Ask Ukraine to Stop Striking Russian Refineries?

President Donald Trump asked Ukraine to stop attacking Russian diesel-producing infrastructure as global diesel supplies tighten. Ukraine's strikes are contributing to the squeeze, but international energy data show they are only one part of a broader supply problem.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 14, 2026 · Multi-source verified synthesis

Trump said Sunday that he had asked Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy to stop targeting Russian refineries and other diesel infrastructure. Reuters and the Associated Press independently reported the remarks, which came after U.S. diesel prices climbed to record levels.

The immediate concern is refined-fuel supply. Ukraine has intensified attacks on Russian refining infrastructure in an effort to reduce revenue and fuel availability that support Russia's war effort. Those attacks have disrupted Russian refining and product exports, tightening an already strained diesel market.

Are Ukraine's refinery strikes causing the diesel shortage?

They are a contributing factor, but the available evidence does not support treating them as the sole cause.

The International Energy Agency's September Oil Market Report says disruptions to Russia's refining system and a near-halt in Russian product exports have compounded much larger disruptions in the Gulf. The IEA estimated that combined net diesel and gasoil exports from Gulf countries and Russia were about 1.6 million barrels per day lower in August than in February. Before those disruptions, the two sources together accounted for almost 45% of global seaborne diesel and gasoil trade.

The Gulf disruption is substantial on its own. The IEA estimated August diesel and gasoil exports from Gulf countries at roughly one-quarter of their pre-war level as traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remained severely constrained. That means the global shortage predates and extends beyond the latest Russian refinery strikes.

How high are U.S. diesel prices?

U.S. Energy Information Administration data put the national average retail diesel price at $5.967 per gallon for the week ending September 7. AP reported that a separate daily price measure moved above $6 per gallon later in the week, setting a record.

Diesel matters beyond drivers who buy it directly. It is widely used by trucks, farm equipment, construction machinery and other freight-dependent industries, so sustained increases can raise transportation and production costs throughout the economy.

Why is Ukraine striking Russian refineries?

Ukraine has described Russian energy infrastructure as part of the economic system financing and supplying Moscow's war. Long-range strikes have therefore become one element of Kyiv's campaign to impose costs inside Russia while Russian forces continue attacks on Ukrainian cities and energy infrastructure.

Trump's request creates a strategic tradeoff: reducing attacks on Russian refining could ease some pressure on global fuel markets, but it could also remove one method Ukraine has used to weaken Russia's wartime energy system.

Would stopping the strikes bring diesel prices down?

Not necessarily, and not by itself. Russian refinery disruptions are one source of the shortage, but the IEA identifies constrained Gulf exports, shipping insecurity, lower global refinery throughput and depleted inventories as additional sources of market tightness.

Prices could respond if Russian refining and exports recovered, but the size and speed of any decline would also depend on Middle East shipping, refinery operations elsewhere, inventories, crude prices and demand. None of those variables is controlled by Ukraine alone.

What remains uncertain: Ukraine has not publicly committed to ending the refinery campaign, and it is unclear whether Trump's request will change Kyiv's targeting decisions. It is also too early to determine how quickly Russian refining could recover or how much that would affect retail diesel prices while Gulf supply disruptions continue.
