What Did the Judge Rule About FEMA’s 50% Staffing Cut?

A federal judge found that the Department of Homeland Security acted unlawfully in directing FEMA toward a 50% workforce reduction. The ruling settles part of the legal dispute, but it does not itself restore thousands of jobs or set FEMA’s final staffing level.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 14, 2026 · Multi-source verified synthesis

U.S. District Judge Susan Illston ruled that the Department of Homeland Security unlawfully took control of staffing decisions that federal law leaves to the Federal Emergency Management Agency and steered FEMA toward cutting its workforce by half. Reuters and the Associated Press independently reported the decision, issued late September 11 in federal court in San Francisco.

What did the court decide?

The core finding concerns who had legal authority over FEMA staffing and how the 50% target was developed. Reuters reported that Illston concluded DHS had usurped FEMA’s personnel authority. AP reported that the judge found senior DHS officials directed FEMA leadership to produce a plan with a 50% staffing reduction despite objections inside the agency.

The ruling also relied on post-Hurricane Katrina protections limiting DHS’s ability to substantially reduce FEMA’s authorities, responsibilities or functions. The court found that the staffing directive violated those protections and that the 50% figure lacked an adequate analytical basis.

Does the ruling immediately restore FEMA jobs?

No. The full 50% reduction was never carried out, and some workers who had previously left or been terminated have since been rehired. The judge did not impose a final remedy in the decision. Instead, the parties were directed to confer over what relief should follow, leaving the practical scope of reinstatement, staffing restrictions or other corrective action unresolved.

Why does FEMA staffing matter?

The ruling arrives against a broader workforce problem documented independently by the Government Accountability Office. GAO reported in August that more than 4,300 employees—about 17% of FEMA’s workforce—separated from the agency in fiscal 2025. FEMA officials told GAO those departures contributed to a loss of experienced personnel and institutional knowledge.

GAO also found that FEMA made workforce reductions and proposed additional changes without a strategic workforce analysis tied to the agency’s present and future mission needs. That matters because FEMA supplies personnel for disaster preparation, response and recovery, so staffing levels affect the agency’s ability to deploy people when major disasters occur.

What happens next?

The immediate question is the remedy. The court’s finding establishes that the challenged DHS-directed 50% staffing plan was unlawful, but the parties still must address what corrective measures the court should order. FEMA told AP that DHS and FEMA were prepared for the 2026 hurricane season and were focused on workforce stability and disaster readiness. DHS had not separately commented on the ruling in the initial Reuters and AP reports.

What remains unresolved: The ruling does not establish a permanent FEMA headcount, and it does not mean that every prior departure or staffing change will be reversed. The extent of court-ordered relief remains to be determined.
