Atlantic Disturbance Has a 40% Development Chance. What Does That Mean?

The National Hurricane Center says a tropical depression could form in the central subtropical Atlantic late this week. Its seven-day formation chance is 40%—but that number does not describe landfall odds or the probability that any particular place will be hit.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 15, 2026
Current NHC outlook: As of the 2 a.m. EDT September 15 outlook, formation odds were near 0% through 48 hours and 40% through seven days. There were no active Atlantic tropical cyclones at that time. Forecasts change; use the latest NHC outlook for current conditions.

What is being watched?

NHC is monitoring a trough of low pressure well east-southeast of Bermuda. Early Tuesday, the agency said showers and thunderstorms remained disorganized, but environmental conditions were expected to become more favorable for development in a few days. NHC said a tropical depression could form late this week while the system moves slowly west-northwestward over the subtropical Atlantic.

What does a 40% formation chance mean?

The percentage in the Tropical Weather Outlook describes the chance that a disturbance will become a tropical cyclone during the stated time period. In this case, NHC assessed a medium, 40% chance of formation within seven days. It is not a forecast that there is a 40% chance of a hurricane striking the United States, Bermuda, or any other location.

A system can develop without ever threatening land. Conversely, a disturbance that has not yet developed can still produce hazardous weather. Formation probability and local impact risk are different questions.

Is there a forecast cone yet?

Not for this disturbance. A forecast cone is associated with an operational tropical cyclone forecast; NHC reported no Atlantic tropical cyclones early September 15. If the disturbance develops and advisories begin, official track and hazard products would provide more useful information about where the system may go.

The cone itself should not be treated as an impact boundary. NHC explains that it represents uncertainty around the forecast track of a cyclone's center. Wind, rain, surge and other hazards can extend outside it.

Why watch this during a below-normal season?

NOAA's August update maintained its expectation for a below-normal 2026 Atlantic hurricane season. A seasonal outlook, however, describes activity across the basin over the season; it does not rule out individual storms. NOAA specifically emphasizes that a below-normal season can still produce dangerous hurricanes and that preparedness remains important.

What should readers watch next?

The useful signals are changes in NHC's two-day and seven-day formation probabilities, whether the disturbance becomes sufficiently organized to be designated a tropical depression, and—only if advisories begin—the official track, intensity and hazard forecasts. Long-range individual model images should not be treated as a deterministic landfall forecast.

For anyone potentially affected later, the appropriate source is the latest National Hurricane Center and local National Weather Service guidance rather than a static article. Rally Point will update this explainer if the system's status materially changes.
