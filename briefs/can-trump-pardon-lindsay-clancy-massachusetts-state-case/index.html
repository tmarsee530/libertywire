Can Trump Pardon Lindsay Clancy in Her Massachusetts State Case?

No. The president's constitutional clemency power applies to federal offenses, not charges brought under Massachusetts law. After Lindsay Clancy's mistrial, decisions about the pending case remain with Massachusetts courts and state authorities.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 14, 2026 · Multi-source verified legal explainer

President Donald Trump said Sunday that the Lindsay Clancy case is a “state situation” after her attorney publicly asked him to consider a pardon. On the central legal question, that distinction is decisive: a U.S. president cannot pardon a state offense.

The Justice Department's Office of the Pardon Attorney says presidential clemency is limited to federal offenses and certain District of Columbia cases. Its guidance states that an offense violating state law is not an “offense against the United States,” the category covered by Article II of the U.S. Constitution.

Why can't the president pardon a Massachusetts charge?

Clancy was prosecuted in Massachusetts state court, not federal court. Reuters reported on September 8 that her lawyer, Kevin Reddington, asked Trump to consider a pardon after her trial ended in a mistrial. Reuters also noted that presidential pardon power applies only to federal crimes.

The Boston Globe reported Monday that Trump responded to the request by describing the matter as a state, rather than federal, situation and said it was something state officials would have to work out. That response matches the Justice Department's published explanation of the limits on presidential clemency.

Who has clemency power in Massachusetts?

Massachusetts assigns state clemency authority to the governor, with the advice and consent of the Governor's Council. The Massachusetts Parole Board, acting as the Advisory Board of Pardons, reviews clemency petitions and may make recommendations to the governor.

That does not mean a gubernatorial pardon can simply end this pending prosecution. Massachusetts' clemency guidance quotes the state constitution as providing that a pardon granted before conviction does not avail a defendant pleading it. Clancy's case ended in a mistrial rather than a conviction, and the state-court process remains unresolved.

What happens next in the Clancy case?

Clancy has pleaded not guilty to state murder charges and asserted a lack-of-criminal-responsibility defense. The first trial ended September 4 after jurors could not reach a unanimous verdict. A mistrial is not a conviction or an acquittal.

The Associated Press reported Friday that the defense has asked the judge to enter an acquittal rather than require a second trial. Prosecutors have argued that the evidence supports criminal responsibility. The judge is expected to consider the defense motion later this month, so the next legal step will come from the Massachusetts court process—not a presidential pardon.

What remains unresolved: No final verdict was reached in the first trial. This brief does not make a finding about Clancy's criminal responsibility, mental state, or the merits of a retrial. Those issues remain for the Massachusetts legal process.

Why the distinction matters

Presidential pardons receive national attention, but the president's clemency authority has a jurisdictional limit. When a case is prosecuted solely under state law, federal presidential clemency cannot erase the state charges. State law and state institutions control the available clemency process.
