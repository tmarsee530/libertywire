What the Hurricane Forecast Cone Really Means

The National Hurricane Center's cone shows uncertainty in the forecast track of a storm's center. It does not show the full area that can experience dangerous wind, rain, surge, or other impacts.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 13, 2026 · Updated September 13, 2026
Key point: A location outside the cone is not automatically safe, and a location inside the cone is not guaranteed to take a direct hit.

What the cone actually represents

The National Hurricane Center says the operational track forecast cone is built around forecast positions of the tropical cyclone's center. For 2026, the size of the circles used to construct the cone is based on the previous five years of official NHC track errors, with the circles sized so that roughly two-thirds of historical forecast errors fall inside them.

That makes the cone a track-uncertainty tool. It is not a boundary line for all hazardous weather.

Why impacts can extend outside it

A tropical cyclone is much larger than the point marking its center. Tropical-storm-force and hurricane-force winds can extend well beyond the forecast track, and rainfall, tornadoes, storm surge, and coastal flooding can affect areas that never fall inside the cone.

For that reason, the NHC publishes additional products for wind probabilities, watches and warnings, storm surge, rainfall, and forecast discussion. The cone is one layer of the forecast, not the whole forecast.

How forecasters build the official track

NHC forecasters use many forms of model guidance rather than simply choosing one computer model. The center's 2026 model summary describes dynamical models, statistical models, statistical-dynamical models, trajectory models, artificial-intelligence and machine-learning models, and ensemble or consensus guidance.

The official forecast is a human-produced synthesis of that guidance and forecaster experience. NHC states that, on average, its official forecasts have smaller errors than any individual model, which is one reason readers should prioritize official NHC products over a single model run circulating on social media.

Why forecasts change

Forecasts are updated as new observations and model guidance arrive. NHC issues official center-position and maximum-wind forecasts every six hours for operational tropical cyclones in the Atlantic and eastern North Pacific, with projections extending out to five days.

A shift in the cone does not mean the earlier forecast was meaningless. It reflects updated information being incorporated into a probabilistic forecast where uncertainty is expected from the beginning.

What is different in 2026?

NHC is also experimenting during the 2026 season with an alternate cone methodology. The experimental graphic uses ellipses instead of circles so that uncertainty in storm speed and uncertainty in storm direction can be represented separately. The standard operational cone remains a separate product.

The experiment is another reminder that the cone is a statistical communication tool. Its shape is based on forecast-error history, not on the physical size of a hurricane.

Why it matters

The simplest way to read a hurricane forecast is to treat the cone as one answer to one question: where might the center go? Then use local National Weather Service and NHC watches, warnings, wind probabilities, surge forecasts, rainfall information, and emergency-management instructions to answer the more important question: what could happen where you are?
