What Are the New Crypto Ethics Rules in the CLARITY Act?

A revised Senate cryptocurrency bill adds conflict-of-interest restrictions for federal officials, judges and spouses, gives state attorneys general a role in enforcement, and would require some significant crypto-related interests to be divested or placed in a blind trust. The proposal is not yet law.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 14, 2026 · Multi-source verified synthesis

The latest version of the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act changes more than the rules for cryptocurrency markets. It also adds a broader ethics framework aimed at conflicts of interest involving public officials and digital assets.

Reuters reported Monday that Senate Republicans released revised bill text containing conflict-of-interest language developed through negotiations involving Republican Sen. Thom Tillis and Democratic Sen. Ruben Gallego. The Associated Press independently reported that President Donald Trump agreed to a substantial portion of that proposal as lawmakers sought support for a key Senate vote.

Who would the proposed crypto ethics rules cover?

According to AP, the bill already included a prohibition on federally elected officials and their spouses, as well as federal judges, issuing digital assets. The revised proposal goes further by addressing certain financial interests connected to entities that issue cryptocurrencies.

AP reported, citing a senior Republican aide familiar with the negotiations, that the updated bill would require a covered person to divest or place in a blind trust a “significant” financial interest in a crypto-issuing entity. Because the legislation is still pending, those requirements are proposals rather than current federal law.

What changed about enforcement?

One of the most consequential revisions is who could enforce the ethics rules. AP reported that the new language gives state attorneys general a role alongside the Justice Department. It would also allow state attorneys general to sue a cryptocurrency exchange that lists a digital asset barred under the legislation.

Reuters separately confirmed that the final draft incorporates new ethics language and that its sponsors described the proposal as giving state authorities a meaningful enforcement role. The change addresses one of the issues that had divided negotiators over whether federal enforcement alone would be sufficient.

What else changed in the CLARITY Act?

The ethics section is only part of a much larger market-structure bill. Reuters reported that the new draft also adds consumer safeguards involving affiliate trading, clarifies the application of state consumer-protection laws and includes protections for certain software developers.

Senate Banking materials describe the broader legislation as an attempt to establish a federal framework for digital-asset markets and define regulatory responsibilities. The bill previously advanced from the Senate Banking Committee in May, and Senate sponsors released updated text ahead of the September floor vote.

What happens next?

The Senate is scheduled to hold a procedural vote on Tuesday, September 15. Clearing that step would allow the legislation to move forward for Senate debate; it would not, by itself, make the CLARITY Act law.

The central open question is whether the revised ethics language and other negotiated changes are enough to produce the votes needed to advance the bill. Lawmakers have continued to disagree over consumer protections, banking-system effects and the adequacy of the conflict-of-interest provisions.

What remains uncertain: The revised CLARITY Act is pending legislation. Its language could still change through the legislative process, the Senate has not yet approved the final bill, and none of the proposed ethics restrictions described here should be treated as current law.
