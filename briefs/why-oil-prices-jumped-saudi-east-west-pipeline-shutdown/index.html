Why Did Oil Prices Jump Nearly 3% After the Saudi Pipeline Shutdown?

Oil prices rose sharply Monday after Saudi Arabia shut its East-West pipeline following attacks, removing an important route around the Strait of Hormuz while risks also increased around other major shipping lanes.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 14, 2026 · Multi-source verified synthesis

Oil markets reacted to a combination of confirmed infrastructure disruption and widening shipping risk. Reuters reported that Brent crude futures were up 2.8% at $107.54 a barrel at 0700 GMT on September 14, while U.S. West Texas Intermediate futures were up 2.9% at $102.93.

The central development was Saudi Arabia's shutdown of the East-West pipeline. Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Energy said the line was shut as a precaution after multiple attacks on September 10. The ministry said technical teams were assessing the pipeline and that further developments would be announced. The Associated Press independently reported the shutdown and described the line as an important export route during disruption in the Strait of Hormuz.

Why does the East-West pipeline matter?

The pipeline carries crude across Saudi Arabia to the Red Sea, giving the kingdom a way to export oil without relying on the Strait of Hormuz. That makes it particularly important when traffic through Hormuz is disrupted or threatened.

Reuters reported that the outage threatens a route associated with as much as roughly 4% of global oil supply. AP reported that Saudi Arabia had more than doubled exports from the Red Sea port at the pipeline's western end earlier this year as it sought alternatives to Hormuz.

What else pushed prices higher?

The pipeline shutdown was not the only factor. Reuters reported a separate strike on a vessel in the Strait of Hormuz and increased risk around Bab el-Mandeb after Houthi forces expanded their position near that shipping corridor. A planned meeting in Oman between Gulf states and Iran on Strait of Hormuz security was also postponed.

Taken together, those developments increased concern that several routes used to move Middle Eastern energy supplies could face disruption at the same time. That does not mean the same amount of oil has already been removed from world supply; markets can move on the risk of future shortages as well as confirmed lost barrels.

Was the pipeline destroyed?

No reliable source reviewed by Rally Point News has established that the East-West pipeline was destroyed. Saudi Arabia's Energy Ministry described the shutdown as precautionary while teams checked its safety after the attacks. Reuters said the extent of damage and how long the line would remain out of service were unclear.

Does this mean oil will keep rising?

That is not established. Oil prices can change quickly as infrastructure is repaired, shipping routes reopen, inventories shift, diplomacy changes or new attacks occur. The verified fact is that prices rose sharply on September 14 as traders reassessed supply risk after the Saudi pipeline shutdown and new threats to regional shipping.

What remains uncertain: Saudi authorities had not announced a timetable for restoring the East-West pipeline when this brief was published. The extent of damage, the duration of the outage and the ultimate effect on physical oil exports remain unresolved. Price figures in this article are time-stamped market observations, not forecasts.
