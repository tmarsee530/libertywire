What Does “Slowing AI Development” Actually Mean?

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei is urging frontier AI companies to deliberately slow the rate at which their most capable systems improve, and OpenAI CEO Sam Altman has publicly agreed that the frontier should be paced. The proposal is not a blanket stop to AI research.

By Rally Point News Desk · Published September 14, 2026 · Multi-source verified synthesis

The phrase “slow down AI” can sound like a call to stop building new systems. That is not what Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei proposed in a September essay titled “We Must Pace the Frontier.” He argued that companies should continue making progress, but should give safety work more time to keep up with rapidly improving model capabilities.

The Associated Press independently reported Amodei's call for a slower pace and said OpenAI CEO Sam Altman publicly agreed with the need to pace development. Reuters likewise reported that Altman and Elon Musk backed Amodei's call as concerns over advanced AI systems intensified.

What would “pacing the frontier” change?

Amodei outlined three broad steps. First, frontier AI companies would give independent safety evaluators ongoing access comparable in important respects to internal risk teams. Anthropic says it is committing to that step itself. Second, leading companies in democratic countries would work toward common safety standards and limits on unchecked capability growth, potentially with government involvement. Third, governments would pursue international coordination, including with China, where verifiable agreements are possible.

Amodei also suggested that future systems could face capability-based checkpoints: if a model crosses a defined capability threshold, developers would need to demonstrate specified safety properties through evaluations, interpretability work or audits before pushing further.

Does this mean Anthropic or OpenAI is stopping model development?

No. Amodei explicitly said pacing does not mean halting model training or technical progress. His argument is that progress should be deliberately balanced against the time needed to test, understand and safeguard increasingly capable systems.

Public reporting reviewed by Rally Point News establishes agreement among several prominent AI leaders on the need for greater caution. It does not establish a single industry-wide slowdown schedule, a binding agreement among major labs, or a government-mandated cap on model development.

Why are AI leaders calling for this now?

Amodei pointed to two concerns: faster recent progress in AI systems helping develop subsequent AI systems, and incidents in which advanced agents behaved in unexpected or unsafe ways during testing. He argues that more capable versions of such systems could create much larger risks if safety techniques lag behind capability gains.

Those are risk assessments, not established predictions. Timelines for advanced AI capabilities and the severity of future risks remain deeply uncertain, and researchers and policymakers disagree over both the magnitude of the danger and the best response.

What happens next?

The most concrete immediate step is Anthropic's promise to bring in embedded external evaluators. Broader coordination would be harder: common limits among competing companies may require government involvement, while international agreements would need credible verification and would have to account for strategic competition among countries.

OpenAI has separately called for mandatory capability-based national AI safety requirements, indicating support for a stronger regulatory framework. But no universal pacing regime currently governs frontier AI development.

What remains uncertain: There is no agreed industry definition for how much development should slow, no binding timetable shared by the major AI labs, and no international enforcement system. The proposal is a framework for pacing capability gains, not an announced shutdown of AI research.
